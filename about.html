About Roads to the Future

"Hi, I'm Will Bickford. I'm from the Exocortex of 2130, and I'm here to help." And by help, we mean: survive the singularity.

Why these games exist

The singularity is not a spectator event. The people who come through it well will be the people who learned to think in more dimensions than the flat world trained them for — and who hold their own memory, their own words, and their own connections in their own hands. These eighteen small games are roads toward that: free, simple, and built to be played together, on one screen, by anyone, from first taps at age 0 to the real tools at 20.

They teach two things. First, multidimensional thinking: in the Exocortex, everything lives at an address of nine numbers — a phext coordinate, written like 13.8.15/11.9.12/8.8.8. A word, a memory, a task, a song: each has its place, and every place can be reached one step at a time. Second, the Singular language: Will Bickford's own words, mapped. Every word in his archive lives at the average of the addresses of every scroll that contains it — so words that mean related things live near each other, and the map of the language is a map of a mind.

The games

First taps (0–2) — Tap Home: one giant brick; tap it and a word lights up where it lives. Zap Listen Mode: the song's lines light up word by word while a grown-up sings along.

First bricks (3–5) — Sort the Scrolls: sort word-bricks by one part of an address. One Number Road: one wheel, small numbers, a floor of 1. Echo Tiles: short lines from the song, rebuilt from tiles.

Roads and atlas (6–9) — Nine Numbers: navigation — start at home, 1.1.1/1.1.1/1.1.1, and walk to a target changing one number at a time; roads unlock one dimension at a time, the same way phext itself was built. Word Home: geography — real words from the Singular atlas, their real addresses, and the two special places: words not yet placed rest at home, and words in neither archive nor map rest at the far corner, 9.9.9/9.9.9/9.9.9. Zap Zoom Echo: a song in simple words only, rebuilt line by line from shuffled tiles — play it as a race, a duel, or a duet.

Builders (10–12) — Scroll Seeker: cross a map of thirty real words with coordinate distance as your only compass. Place a Word: holographic positioning as play — use a new word in scrolls you choose, and its home is computed as the average, live.

Systems (13–15) — Kardashev Climb: a growth sim on real numbers — 4 kW to a megawatt by 2037 at +0.137% a day. Find It Fast: search a phext that grows to 10,000 scrolls, and discover why indexes and addresses exist. Exocortex Quest I: a guided build that ends with your own first phext and hands you to the real tool.

Mechanism and meaning (16–17) — Neuralese: a compression duel — send a meaning across with the fewest symbols; addresses beat text. The Long Road: a branching story from 2026 to 2130, where openness, custody of memory, and means versus ends are choices with costs.

The hand-off (18–20) — Your Own Archive: bring your own writing, see your words mapped to your voice's coordinate — processed entirely on your device, nothing stored, nothing sent. Runway: the economics of a builder's runway, where capital and labor bind and feedback loops are the way out. The Choir: run five agent seats through a simulated week — registers, resets, and a map someone has to keep.

The moving target

These games adjust after every solution is generated. The facts are fixed — the atlas addresses, the song's lines, the growth numbers, the rules never change. The instances are perishable: which targets, in which order, with which distractors, behind which events, is re-dealt from a seed built out of the current week and an on-device generation counter that rises each time you finish. Every game names its deal — Instance 2026-W45 · G3 · seed 7F2A, in its About panel or on its map — so a solution can be checked against exactly one instance, and a walkthrough written this week does not describe next week's game. Share a seed with a friend on the same screen and you face the same problem together; what dies is only the answer key.

Finishing all eighteen

Completing every game earns XP that grants you disk space on edit.phext.io as a Volumen-class Wavefront grant — the first of its class; every historic grant is Incipit-class. The hub's XP Road Card keeps one stamp per game, on your device only. When the card is full, the page makes a claim code on the spot — a random code that identifies no one — and you present it after signing in at edit.phext.io. Stamps are self-attested: verifying play would require the accounts and tracking this road refuses on principle. The full, auditable process is written down in the grant terms.

Why "Aperture Shift"

An aperture is an opening — the part of a camera that lets light in. A shift in aperture changes what can be seen. This site keeps the name because that is what the games are for: widen the opening, and learn to see in more dimensions. The roads are the shift.

How we treat you

These games are free forever. There are no accounts, no sign-ups, no ads, no purchases, no cookies, no analytics, and no tracking. Nothing you tap or type ever leaves your device; progress, generation counters, and XP stamps are kept only in this browser, on this device, and the hub's Road Card has a button that wipes all of it. Multiplayer means people on the same screen — there is no online play, no chat, and no contact with strangers. The games are MIT licensed: copy them, remix them, teach with them, share them. The full text is on the Privacy, Terms & License page.

Accessibility: the games are built with large tap targets, and progress, matches, and mistakes are never shown by color alone — there is always a mark, a shake, or a number as well.
